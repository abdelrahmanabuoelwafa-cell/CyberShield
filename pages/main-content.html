Topic 1

Cybersecurity Introduction

Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting systems, networks, devices and data from digital attacks. For a normal user it boils down to three questions: who can access my accounts, who can read my data, and who can run code on my devices. Every topic on this page is one practical answer to those questions.

The scale is easy to underestimate. An attacker does not need to target you personally; automated tools scan the entire internet for weak passwords and unpatched devices around the clock. Good security is not about being unhackable - it is about being expensive enough to attack that opportunistic attackers move on.

  • Confidentiality - only the right people can read the data.
  • Integrity - the data is not secretly altered.
  • Availability - the data and services are there when needed.

These three goals are the classic CIA triad that every security course starts from. Keep them in mind and the reasoning behind every habit below becomes obvious.

Topic 2

Password Security

Passwords remain the front door of almost every account, and password reuse is the habit that turns one small breach into a chain of account takeovers. When a site you use is breached, attackers test the leaked email/password pair on banking, email and social platforms automatically - this is called credential stuffing.

What makes a password strong

  • Long: 12+ characters minimum; length adds strength faster than symbol soup.
  • Unique: one password per account, so a single leak stays contained.
  • Random: no names, birthdays, qwerty patterns or "P@ssw0rd" mutations.
  • Stored safely: in a password manager, not in a notes app or a browser text file.

Passphrases and 2FA

A passphrase such as correct-horse-battery-staple style word combinations is both easier to remember and far harder to crack than Abc123!. Add two-factor authentication (2FA) and a stolen password alone is no longer enough to enter the account - the attacker also needs your phone or authenticator app.

Topic 3

Phishing Awareness

Phishing is a social-engineering attack: instead of breaking software, the attacker tricks you into giving access away. The classic delivery is an urgent email that impersonates your bank, university or a delivery service and pushes you to a fake login page. Variants target phones (vishing) and SMS (smishing).

The defense is a habit, not a product: slow down and inspect. No legitimate organization needs your password over email, and real urgency is rare.

Red flags of a phishing attempt

SignWhat it looks likeWhat to do
Sender mismatchDisplay name says "Microsoft" but the address is a look-alike domain (rnicrosoft.com, micros0ft-support.net).Check the full address, not just the display name.
Manufactured urgency"Your account will be deleted in 24 hours" - pressure to act before thinking.Treat urgency itself as the suspicious signal.
Generic greeting"Dear Customer" instead of your actual name from an institution that knows it.Real institutions that hold your data personalize.
Link mismatchThe visible text says "paypal.com" but hovering reveals a different URL.Hover first, click never - type the address yourself.
Unexpected attachmentAn invoice or "scanned document" (.zip, .exe, macro-enabled Office file) you never asked for.Do not open; verify through the official channel.

Topic 4

Data Protection

Data protection is about limiting who can reach your personal files and being able to recover them when something goes wrong. Ransomware, theft, a failed disk or a spilled coffee all end the same way if the only copy of your data lived on that one device.

The 3-2-1 backup rule

  • 3 copies of important data (original + two backups).
  • 2 different media types (laptop + external drive, for example).
  • 1 copy off-site (cloud storage), safe from theft and physical damage.

Privacy hygiene

Reduce what you expose in the first place: review app permissions, limit what social profiles show publicly, and think of every photo or document you upload as permanent. On laptops and phones, full-disk encryption (BitLocker, FileVault) makes a stolen device a loss of hardware, not a leak of your life.

Topic 5

Safe Internet Practices

Everyday browsing decisions decide your exposure. The padlock icon and https:// in the address bar mean traffic to that site is encrypted - it does not mean the site is honest; scammers use HTTPS too. What it does prevent is anyone on the network between you and the site from reading or altering the data.

Public Wi-Fi is convenient but shared: on an open network, use HTTPS sites only and prefer your mobile hotspot or a VPN for anything sensitive. Software updates are the other half of safety - most real-world attacks exploit bugs that already had a fix available, sometimes for months.
